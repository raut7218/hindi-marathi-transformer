Deep Architectural Synthesis for Hindi-Marathi Neural Machine Translation: Pretraining Bidirectional and Autoregressive Representations with RoPE, GQA, and RMSNorm
The modern landscape of Natural Language Processing has undergone a fundamental shift toward the utilization of large-scale pretrained representations. In the context of the AdiVaani Initiative at MISN Lab, IIT Delhi, the objective of developing robust neural machine translation systems for underrepresented and morphologically rich languages like Hindi and Marathi necessitates a move away from classical recurrent architectures toward highly optimized Transformer-based models.1 This report provides an exhaustive analysis of the research and implementation strategies required to construct an advanced translation framework from the ground up, utilizing a bidirectional BERT-like encoder and an autoregressive GPT-style decoder, both incorporating state-of-the-art architectural modifications to enhance performance, efficiency, and stability.
Theoretical Foundations of Modern Transformer Block Modifications
The original Transformer architecture, while revolutionary, introduced several bottlenecks in terms of positional awareness, memory efficiency during inference, and computational overhead in normalization layers.2 To address these issues, the current research prioritizes three primary architectural enhancements: Rotary Positional Embeddings, Grouped Query Attention, and Root Mean Square Layer Normalization.
Rotary Positional Embeddings: A Comprehensive Derivation
Traditional positional encoding methods, such as the sinusoidal encodings introduced in the seminal "Attention Is All You Need" paper, rely on additive vectors that are injected into the input embeddings.4 While elegant, these methods lack a direct mathematical relationship between the attention scores and the relative distance between tokens. Furthermore, learned absolute positional embeddings, as used in the original BERT and GPT-2 models, are constrained by a fixed maximum sequence length, failing to extrapolate to longer contexts.6

Memory and Bandwidth Optimization via Grouped Query Attention
As the demand for longer context windows and higher inference throughput increases, the memory bandwidth required to load the Key-Value (KV) cache becomes the primary performance bottleneck in autoregressive models like GPT-2.15 In standard Multi-Head Attention (MHA), every query head has a corresponding unique set of key and value heads. For a model with 12 heads and a hidden dimension of 768, this requires storing and loading 12 key and 12 value projections for every token in the sequence.15
Grouped Query Attention (GQA) provides a generalized optimization by allowing multiple query heads to share a single key-value head.19 By partitioning the    query heads into    groups, the KV cache size is reduced by a factor of   .15

Computational Stability through Root Mean Square Layer Normalization
Normalization is an indispensable component of deep neural networks, serving to stabilize the distribution of activations and gradients throughout the model stack.23 The standard Layer Normalization (LayerNorm) operation involves two distinct steps: recentering (subtracting the mean) and rescaling (dividing by the standard deviation).23
Recent empirical research has suggested that the mean-centering operation in LayerNorm is computationally redundant and provides negligible benefit to the overall expressivity of the model.26 Root Mean Square Layer Normalization (RMSNorm) simplifies the process by performing only the rescaling operation based on the root mean square of the activations:

Pretraining Methodology: Designing the MLM and CLM Pipelines
The effectiveness of neural machine translation is heavily dependent on the quality of the pretrained representations. This project requires training the models from scratch on monolingual and parallel corpora.1
Masked Language Modeling (MLM) for the Encoder
The encoder is trained using a Masked Language Modeling objective, which forces the model to understand the bidirectional context of a sentence.42 For this research, the Next Sentence Prediction (NSP) objective is explicitly removed, as modern studies have shown that training on single long sequences leads to better downstream performance on most tasks.42
The MLM pipeline is designed with the following selection policy:
1. Selection: 15% of tokens in the input sequence are chosen for masking.42
2. Replacement: 80% of selected tokens are replaced with a special `` token, 10% are replaced with a random token from the vocabulary, and 10% remain unchanged.42
3. Dynamic Masking: To prevent the model from memorizing specific mask patterns, the masking is generated on-the-fly for every training iteration.42
4. Whole Word and Span Masking: Given the rich morphology of Marathi and Hindi, masking individual subwords can lead to "leakage," where the model predicts a suffix based on a visible stem.48 Implementing whole-word masking (masking all subword units of a single word) or span masking (masking contiguous spans of tokens) increases the difficulty of the task and encourages the model to rely on higher-level syntactic and semantic cues.44
Causal Language Modeling (CLM) for the Decoder
The GPT-style decoder is trained using a Causal Language Modeling objective, where the model predicts the next token in a sequence given the previous context.50 This is implemented using a causal mask that prevents tokens from attending to future positions.6 The loss function is the standard cross-entropy between the predicted logit distribution and the actual next token.51
For Hindi-to-Marathi translation, the decoder must be pretrained on high-quality Marathi text to ensure that the generated output is fluent and grammatically correct.53 The use of the "Three-Phase Transformer" structural prior may be considered, which partitions the hidden vector into cyclic channels to maintain phase-respecting operations, though the assessment requirements prioritize GQA and RoPE.29
Solving the Sequence-to-Sequence Translation Challenge
After pretraining the encoder and decoder components independently, the core challenge involves integrating them into a unified machine translation framework.
Integration through Cross-Attention Initialization
The most principled methodology for combining a BERT-like encoder and a GPT-style decoder is through a "warm-started" encoder-decoder framework.55 However, a standard GPT decoder does not contain the cross-attention layers required to receive information from the encoder.3
Implementation steps for the translation bridge:
1. Augmenting the Decoder: Each block of the GPT-style decoder must be modified to include a cross-attention sublayer between the self-attention and FFN sublayers.56
2. Random Initialization: Since the pretrained GPT weights do not include cross-attention, these new layers are initialized randomly.57
3. Bridge Dynamics: In cross-attention, the Queries (  ) are derived from the decoder's self-attention output, while the Keys (  ) and Values (  ) are derived from the encoder's final hidden states.3 This allows the decoder to "look back" at the Hindi source sentence while generating the Marathi target sentence.
The Problem of Representational Compatibility
A critical failure case in combining independently pretrained models is representational mismatch.60 If the BERT encoder learns a semantic space that is fundamentally different in scale or distribution from the GPT decoder, the cross-attention layers may fail to converge.62 To mitigate this, a "Dynamic Switching Gate" or a small intermediate Feed-Forward Network (adapter) can be placed between the encoder and decoder to map the representations into a compatible space.62
Furthermore, "Asymptotic Distillation" can be employed during the initial phase of translation training.60 This involves an auxiliary loss that penalizes the model if its internal representations drift too far from the original pretrained checkpoints, ensuring that the "knowledge" acquired during pretraining is preserved while the model learns to translate.63
Linguistic Modeling for Hindi and Marathi
Hindi and Marathi are Indo-Aryan languages that share the Devanagari script and exhibit significant morphological and syntactic overlap.13 However, they possess distinct case systems, gender agreement rules, and verbal paradigms that the model must generalize across.
Tokenization Strategy: SentencePiece vs. BPE
For these languages, tokenization is the foundation of effective representation.64 While Byte-Pair Encoding (BPE) is standard for English, SentencePiece is widely considered superior for morphologically rich Indian languages.66

Data Preparation and Preprocessing
The Hindi-Marathi parallel corpus provided for the assignment must be filtered for misalignments, which are common in crawled datasets like Samanantar or OPUS.21 Preprocessing should include Unicode NFKC normalization and the removal of noise.69 Sampling a representative subset of the data is permissible, but ensuring linguistic diversity (e.g., news, legal, and colloquial text) is essential for the model's generalization capabilities.53

The assignment requires the efficient use of available GPU resources.1 Training 100M+ parameter models is memory-intensive; therefore, Mixed Precision (FP16 or BF16) should be enabled.17 BF16 is particularly advantageous as it offers the same dynamic range as FP32, significantly reducing the risk of overflow during the training of deep architectures like the 12-layer Transformer.10
Distributed Data Parallel (DDP) is the preferred framework for multi-GPU setups.17 Unlike Fully Sharded Data Parallel (FSDP), DDP is more efficient for models of this scale (under 1B parameters), as the entire model fits within a single GPU's VRAM, avoiding unnecessary communication overhead.17
Failure Analysis and Critical Reasoning
In a research-oriented assessment, identifying why a model fails is as important as its success. Several potential failure modes must be monitored.
Logit Explosion and Softmax Saturation
A common issue in training modernized Transformers is the drift of query and key norms, which causes attention scores to explode.10 When    reaches high values, the softmax function becomes extremely "sharp," assigning a probability of 1.0 to a single token.10 This results in zero gradients for all other tokens, effectively halting the learning process.10 This "logit chasing" is mitigated by the scale-anchoring properties of RMSNorm and the use of QK-Normalization or proper scaling by   .24
Catastrophic Forgetting and Mismatch
When the decoder is fine-tuned for translation, there is a risk that it will "forget" the fluent Marathi language modeling learned during pretraining and instead produce "translationese"—text that follows Hindi syntax but uses Marathi words.60 This is analyzed by comparing the validation loss of the translation model against a baseline Marathi language model. If the translation model's perplexity on target Marathi text is significantly higher than the LM baseline, it indicates a failure to leverage the pretrained representations.60
Subword Segmentation Artifacts
If the SentencePiece vocabulary is too small, common Marathi inflections may be broken into many meaningless characters, causing the model to struggle with long-range morphological dependencies.21 Conversely, a vocabulary that is too large may result in "sparse" embeddings where many tokens are rarely updated, leading to poor generalization for rare words.21 The optimal "Goldilocks" zone for these languages is typically found between 32k and 50k tokens.21
Performance Evaluation and Metrics
The final system must be evaluated using quantitative and qualitative measures.1
   1. Quantitative Metrics:
   * BLEU (Bilingual Evaluation Understudy): Measures the overlap of n-grams between the generated translation and the reference.67 Results should be reported on a 0-100 scale.1
   * CHRF++: A character-level F-score that is particularly effective for morphologically rich languages like Marathi, as it captures subword-level correctness even when the exact word form is slightly off.67
   2. Qualitative Analysis:
   * Example translations should be examined to identify if the model correctly handles gender agreement (e.g., "Hindi: वह जाता है" vs. "Marathi: तो जातो") and case marking (e.g., ergative 'ne' in Hindi vs. Marathi equivalents).14
   3. Visualization:
   * Train/Validation Loss plots should be analyzed for signs of overfitting or divergence.1
   * BLEU-100 and CHRF++-100 plots provide a direct view of the model's learning trajectory on the translation task.1
Future Improvements and Scalability
While the current research focuses on a 110M/124M parameter configuration, the architectural choices (RoPE, GQA, RMSNorm) are designed for scalability.10 Future iterations could explore:
   * Larger Models: Scaling to 1B+ parameters using the same backbone.15
   * Advanced Masking: Implementing SpanBERT-style span-boundary objectives to better capture Marathi's complex verbal clusters.44
   * Multimodal Integration: Leveraging the AdiVaani Initiative's broader focus on indigenous languages by integrating speech-to-text features via cross-modal adapters.51
The proposed framework represents a rigorous, theoretically grounded approach to solving the Hindi-Marathi translation problem. By synthesizing bidirectional contextual understanding with autoregressive generation efficiency, and utilizing modern architectural optimizations, the resulting system is poised to deliver high-quality, linguistically nuanced translations while remaining computationally efficient and stable. This deep architectural synthesis not only meets the requirements of the assessment but also establishes a foundation for ongoing research in low-resource Indic NLP.
